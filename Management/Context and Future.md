# Context and Future

Brisbane 2032 is a mega-event, yet it is also a live experiment in how the business of sport in Australia may evolve. The Olympic and Paralympic Games will take place across an unusually dispersed venue network: 37 competition sites for 28 Olympic and 22 Paralympic sports, spread across Brisbane, the Gold Coast, the Sunshine Coast, regional Queensland and even interstate locations (GIICA, 2025). This scale creates a fan experience challenge that extends far beyond the action inside the arena. Spectators will queue, navigate transport hubs, wait for sessions to begin, fill breaks in play and encounter sports they may not fully understand. These moments shape the Games just as much as the medal events.

Brisbane 2032 is also positioned as a legacy project. The Queensland Government frames the Games as a catalyst for long-term social, economic and wellbeing benefits, supporting sustainable growth, stronger communities and increased participation (Queensland Government, n.d.). This matters for Play the Games because the concept is more than an entertainment layer. It responds to a strategic question: how can the Games deliver a richer, more commercially valuable and more educational fan experience without relying on heavy infrastructure?

Attention is the core challenge. Live sport once commanded attention by default because stadiums and broadcasts offered few alternatives. That era has shifted. Digital and social media are woven into everyday behaviour. DataReportal (2025) reports 20.9 million active social media identities in Australia, representing 77.9% of the population, and 80.2% of internet users engage with at least one platform. This does not mean every spectator will scroll during every break, yet it shows that the strongest competitor for any idle moment is already in their pocket. The battle for Olympic attention sits between the Games and TikTok, Instagram, YouTube, messaging apps, short-form video and mobile gaming.

This shift is especially visible inside venues. Deloitte (2024) found that 82% of sports fans use their phones during live events. Many want more: 58% wish they had access to the same statistics, analysis and replays they enjoy at home. At the same time, 68% feel people use their phones too much. This tension reveals something important. Fans do not reject phones. They reject phone use that pulls them away from the live moment. For Brisbane 2032, the opportunity lies in redirecting phone use back into the Olympic experience.

Play the Games builds on this behavioural reality. The concept uses the phone to beat the scroll. Instead of letting dead time drift into external platforms, the Games can reclaim these moments through short sponsor-funded mobile games, rule-based quizzes, section leaderboards and redeemable rewards. Engagement theory reinforces this logic. Engagement is interactive value co-creation between customers and a brand or ecosystem (Brodie et al., 2011). A static logo may be noticed. An interactive challenge can be played, remembered, redeemed, measured and shared. The fan becomes a participant rather than an observer.

The Australian market is well suited to this approach. IGEA (2023) reports that 81% of Australians play video games and 94% of households have at least one gaming device. The typical player is a mainstream consumer across age and gender. This supports a lightweight, low-barrier game mechanic at Brisbane 2032. Australians already understand games. The design question is whether the experience is short, social, accessible and closely connected to the live event.

Gamification research strengthens this point. Hamari et al. (2014) show that gamification can produce positive effects when context and implementation are carefully aligned. Play the Games should therefore avoid generic systems. It should reflect the emotional and commercial logic of live sport: anticipation, competition, social comparison, surprise, reward and collective atmosphere. A stadium screen showing a leaderboard or winner creates a shared crowd moment from a private phone interaction.

Commercial dynamics are evolving as well. Sponsorship is shifting from passive visibility to measurable activation. The global sports sponsorship market is projected to grow from USD 70.2 billion in 2025 to USD 96.45 billion in 2030, driven by digital integration, personalised fan experiences and data-driven ROI (The Business Research Company, 2026). This is highly relevant for Brisbane 2032, where domestic sponsorship, ticketing and licensing are expected to generate AUD 3.5 billion, alongside an IOC contribution of AUD 1.4 billion (Queensland Parliament, 2022). Dead-time attention becomes commercial inventory in this environment.

Comparable innovation already exists outside sport. REDyPLAY transforms cinema pre-show time into interactive big-screen gaming for brands, including competitions, loyalty mechanics and a “hero moment” where the winner appears on the screen (Weischer, n.d.). A LEGO campaign using REDyPLAY ran across 196 cinema screens in Germany, with prizes redeemable at the concession counter (SAWA, 2022). A stadium is more complex than a cinema, yet the underlying principle transfers: when an audience is present, waiting and screen-facing, short interactive games can convert passive attention into measurable engagement.

For Brisbane 2032, this creates a forward-looking opportunity. The Games can become a proof of concept for a new Australian fan engagement model that is sponsor-funded, mobile-first, venue-enabled and commercially measurable. The model is attractive because it is infrastructure-light. It can begin with QR codes, a progressive web app, stadium screen integration, simple leaderboards and digital vouchers. Later, it can evolve into ticket-app integration, NFC entry, cross-venue leaderboards, personalised content and richer sponsor dashboards.

The future of live sport will be hybrid. Fans will continue to attend events for atmosphere, identity and collective emotion. They will also expect digital layers that make the experience easier to understand, easier to share and more personally rewarding. Play the Games responds to this future by turning dead time into game time and game time into engagement, revenue and learning. The strategic value lies in giving Brisbane 2032 a mechanism to keep attention inside the Olympic ecosystem, help spectators understand unfamiliar sports, create measurable sponsor value and build a platform that can continue beyond the Games.
