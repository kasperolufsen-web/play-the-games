# Persona 1
Liam O’Connor — The Multi-Event Games Fan

## Personal Information

Age: 39 \
Gender: Male \
Location: Brisbane-based, originally from regional Queensland \
Occupation: Project manager / senior analyst / engineering consultant \
Income situation: Stable middle to upper-middle income; willing to spend on meaningful live experiences rather than everyday luxury
Olympic behaviour: Attends several Brisbane 2032 sessions with his partner, brother and friends. He has tickets for one mainstream sport and two sports he rarely watches. \
Hobbies: Live sport, running, cycling, gym, pub trivia, fantasy football/tipping competitions, Olympic documentaries, podcasts, travel planning, food markets, craft beer with friends, collecting event merchandise and limited-edition memorabilia. \
Phone behaviour: Uses his phone for tickets, maps, transport, group chats, fantasy sport/tipping apps, live stats, medal tables, athlete profiles and quick event updates. \
Sport knowledge: Knows AFL, NRL, cricket and tennis well, but is less familiar with Olympic disciplines such as canoe slalom, fencing, artistic gymnastics scoring or modern pentathlon. \
Mindset: “I’m not just here for one event. I want to feel like I’m part of the whole Games.”

## Why Liam is a strong main persona

Liam is not meant to represent “most men in the stadium”. He represents a high-value Olympic fan situation: someone who is already motivated enough to attend multiple sessions, but needs a more connected experience across sports, venues and days.

That makes him useful because he validates the features that go beyond a one-off QR game:
- Account Mode
- points across sessions
- cross-venue leaderboard
- Olympic trivia
- sport history content
- larger cumulative rewards
- repeat engagement

He is also a bridge to the post-2032 business case. After Brisbane 2032, a person like Liam can easily become the domestic sport user at AFL, NRL, cricket or tennis events.

## Pain points

Liam is already interested in sport, so his problem is not basic boredom. His problem is fragmentation.

He is attending multiple sessions, but the experience feels split across different tickets, venues, sports and days. He checks schedules, messages friends, looks up athletes and follows medals, but there is no simple mechanism that connects his Games journey into one continuous experience.

During dead time, he still reaches for his phone, but without Play the Games, that phone use often takes him outside the Olympic ecosystem.

## What Play the Games does for him

Play the Games gives Liam a reason to stay engaged between sessions and during breaks. He creates an account, collects points across events, competes with friends and other sections, answers Olympic trivia, joins prediction games and tracks his progress on a cross-venue leaderboard.

For Liam, Play the Games is not just “something to do while waiting”. It becomes a Games companion.

## Persona-specific Ask

Think: “This connects my whole Olympic experience, not just one session.” \
Feel: “I am part of the Games, not just watching them.” \
Do: Create an account, collect points across sessions, join leaderboards, compete with friends and return to play at the next venue. \
Proof / KPI: Account Mode opt-in rate, repeat play across sessions, cross-venue leaderboard participation, points collected per user, return rate between events.

# Persona 2
Sofia — The First-Time Olympic Sport Viewer

## Personal Information

Name: Sofia Müller \
Age: 31 \
Gender: Female \
Location: Visiting Brisbane from Germany / Europe \
Occupation: Consultant / teacher / young professional \
Income situation: Has saved for the trip; treats Brisbane 2032 as a once-in-a-lifetime experience \
Olympic behaviour: Attends two or three events, including at least one unfamiliar sport \
Hobbies: Travel, photography, culture, food, fitness classes, international events, light social media storytelling \
Phone behaviour: Uses her phone for maps, translation, photos, ticketing, quick searches and messaging \
Sport knowledge: Understands the Olympics as a global event, but does not know the rules or scoring logic of every sport \
Mindset: “I’m excited to be here, but I don’t always understand what I’m watching.”

## Why Sofia is a strong main persona

Sofia is stronger than a generic “International Visitor” because the core problem is not nationality. The real situation is: a spectator has paid to attend an Olympic sport but does not fully understand the rules, scoring or athlete context.

This is highly plausible for Brisbane 2032 because the Games involve a very broad multi-sport environment. Our context chapter states that Brisbane 2032 covers 37 competition sites for 28 Olympic and 22 Paralympic sports across several regions, which creates fan experience moments where spectators wait, move between venues and encounter sports they may not fully understand.

Our current persona list already describes the First-Time Olympic Sport Viewer as someone who bought tickets to an unfamiliar sport, has no context for rules, scoring or athletes, feels lost during dead time and is motivated by learning what is going on.

The gaming/esports course material strengthens this logic because it frames gaming not only as entertainment, but also as a communication and learning channel. This supports the idea of learning disguised as play.

## Pain Points

Sofia is physically present but not fully cognitively connected. She sees the atmosphere, hears the crowd and watches the athletes, but she may not understand why a score changed, why someone was penalised or why a certain moment was impressive.

During dead time, this confusion becomes more dangerous. If nothing helps her understand the sport, she may default to photos, messages, translation apps or social media. The Games remain memorable, but the sport itself stays distant.

## What Play the Games does for her

Play the Games gives Sofia a simple way to understand the sport in the moment. She scans a QR code, selects her language and plays a 60–120 second rule quiz or “what just happened?” challenge. Instead of reading a long explanation, she learns through interaction.

For example: \
“Why did the athlete receive a penalty?” \
A: Time violation \
B: Lane infringement \
C: Equipment fault \
D: False start

After the answer, she gets a short explanation and a small reward. When the same situation happens again, she understands the live event better.

## Persona-specific Ask

Think: “Now I understand what I am watching.” \
Feel: “I feel included, confident and less out of place.” \
Do: Scan the QR code, choose her language, complete a short rule explainer or quiz, redeem a small reward and return to the live sport. \
Proof / KPI: Guest Mode start rate, quiz completion rate, language selection usage, self-reported understanding, reward redemption rate, re-engagement with the event.

# Persona 3
Jack — The Post-2032 Domestic Sports Fan

## Personal Information

Name: Jack Williams \
Age: 29 \
Gender: Male \
Location: Brisbane local \
Occupation: Apprentice electrician / sales representative / office worker \
Income situation: Budget-conscious but regularly spends on sport, food and drinks with friends \
Sport behaviour: Attends NRL, AFL, cricket or football with mates \
Hobbies: Fantasy sport, pub trivia, gym, BBQs, NRL/AFL tipping, console gaming, weekend events \
Phone behaviour: Checks messages, scores, sports news or social feeds during breaks \
Mindset: “I know my sport, but halftime and queues are still dead time.”

## Why this belongs in the appendix

He is mainly relevant for the post-2032 commercialisation story. He is not the best proof for the Olympic launch context, because his strongest use case is recurring Australian sport after the Games.

He represents the fan situation that becomes important once Play the Games moves from Brisbane 2032 into AFL, NRL, Cricket Australia, A-League, tennis or other domestic live-event contexts. Our implementation chapter already describes this exact scaling logic: after the Games, the platform should become a legacy product licensed to Australian sporting codes, venue groups and event owners.

Strategically, Jack proves that the concept is not Olympic-only. The same mechanics (dead time, screen prompts, QR scan, leaderboard, sponsor reward, concession redemption) can work at recurring sports events where fans already understand the sport but still experience downtime.

## Pain Points

Jack is not confused by his sport. His pain is different from Sofia’s.

His main pain points are:
- Dead time feels wasted: Halftime, pre-game waiting, queues and breaks are moments where his attention drifts.
- Existing sponsor exposure feels passive: LED boards, static signage and naming rights are visible, but they rarely create interaction.
- Queues reduce enjoyment: Waiting for food or drinks feels like a break from the experience rather than part of it.
- No reason to engage digitally with the venue: His phone use usually goes to group chats, social media or external sports apps.
- Friends are part of the experience: If an activation does not feel social or competitive, it is less likely to matter to him.
- Rewards need to be immediate: Long-term loyalty points are less motivating than something he can use now, such as a drink discount, food offer or merch deal.

## What Play the Games does for him

For Jack, Play the Games turns ordinary stadium downtime into a short, competitive, social moment.

During halftime or a break, a QR code appears on the stadium screen: “Play for your section — win rewards before the second half.” Jack scans it with his friends. He does not need to download an app or create an account. He joins a fast challenge linked to the match, the team, or the sponsor.

The value for him is immediate:
- He competes against friends or other sections.
- He can win food, drink or merch discounts.
- He sees live leaderboard updates on the stadium screen.
- He has a reason to stay inside the venue ecosystem instead of drifting - to social media.
- He is encouraged to redeem rewards immediately, creating concession and merchandise uplift.

For the business case, Jack shows that Play the Games can become a repeatable fan engagement layer for domestic sport. He does not need Olympic education content. He needs competition, mateship, immediate rewards and a reason to stay engaged during breaks.

## Persona-specific Ask

Think: “Halftime and queues are not dead time anymore.” \
Feel: “This is fun, competitive and worth doing with my mates.” \
Do: Play during breaks, compete with friends or sections, redeem food/drink/merch offers and return at future events. \
Proof / KPI: Participation during breaks, concession voucher redemption, repeat use at later domestic events, sponsor package renewal.

# Persona 4
Aaliyah — The Social Sharer

## Personal Information

Name: Aaliyah Nguyen \
Age: 22 \
Gender: Female \
Location: Brisbane / Gold Coast \
Occupation: University student / part-time retail worker \
Income situation: Budget-conscious; strongly motivated by free rewards and social experiences \
Hobbies: TikTok, Instagram, concerts, food pop-ups, festivals, group chats, short-form video, casual sport events \
Phone behaviour: Documents moments worth sharing \
Mindset: “If something cool happens, I’ll post it.”

## Why this belongs in the appendix

Aaliyah should not be a main persona because we cannot prove that most spectators are “social media natives.” She is better used as a shareability stress test.

The evidence should be framed as stadium behaviour, not demographic dominance. Deloitte found that 82% of sports fans use their mobile phones in some way at live professional sporting events, while around a quarter use mobile devices to post to social media during the event. This supports the existence of the behaviour, but not that this group dominates the stadium.

Your own concept already includes shareable mechanics: leaderboard moments, winners on the stadium screen, prize wins and organic word of mouth after play.

## Pain Points

Aaliyah’s pain points are less about understanding sport and more about attention, relevance and shareability.

Her main pain points are:
- Dead time immediately becomes phone time: If nothing happens, she will scroll or post unrelated content.
- Most sponsor activations feel boring: Static logos, banners or generic ads do not feel worth noticing or sharing.
- The event does not always give her digital moments: A good live atmosphere does not automatically translate into shareable content.
- Rewards must feel instant and visible: If the reward is hidden, delayed or complicated, she loses interest.
- She wants social proof: Being ranked, recognised, featured or rewarded publicly is more motivating than a private coupon alone.
- She has low patience for friction: App downloads, long forms or account creation can break the experience.

## What Play the Games does for her

For Aaliyah, Play the Games creates moments that are both interactive and shareable.

She scans a QR code because the stadium screen creates urgency: “Play now — winner appears on the big screen.” She enters through Guest Mode, plays a short challenge and sees her score or section ranking update live. If she wins, receives a badge, appears on a leaderboard or unlocks a reward, the moment becomes social content.

The product gives her:
- a quick, low-friction activity;
- visual proof of participation;
- shareable leaderboard or winner moments;
- instant rewards;
- a reason to stay connected to the live event instead of leaving it digitally.

For the business case, Aaliyah proves the earned-media potential. If Play the Games creates moments people want to post, then individual participation can become organic reach for the event, the sponsor and the platform.

## Persona-specific Ask

Think: “This is worth sharing.” \
Feel: “I want people to see that I won, ranked or appeared on the screen.” \
Do: Share leaderboard moments, winner badges or reward wins on social media. \
Proof / KPI: Share button usage, tagged posts, referral scans from shared links, user-generated content, organic reach.
