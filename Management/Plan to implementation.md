## Plan to implementation / commercialisation (Natascha)
The implementation of Play the Games should follow a land‑and‑expand logic. The aim is to begin with a controlled environment, prove the mechanic, gather evidence, reduce operational risk and then grow through event owners, venue partners and brand sponsors. This approach matters because the concept sits at the intersection of three groups: fans, brands and venues. Fans are the users. Brands are the paying customers because they seek measurable attention and redemption behaviour. Venues and event owners are the strategic gatekeepers who shape the physical environment in which that attention emerges.
Commercialisation should reflect this balance. Venues should not carry upfront costs. A clearer model is straightforward: brands fund it, venues enable it, fans use it and value is shared. Venues already monetise sponsorship through signage, naming rights, screen placements, hospitality and activation booths. Play the Games adds a new category of inventory that focuses on interactive, measurable and reward‑based sponsor engagement during dead time. Brands gain access to metrics such as scans, players, completion rates, time spent, leaderboard participation, reward redemption and follow‑up conversion. Venues gain additional sponsorship inventory, improved fan experience, uplift in concessions and merchandise and privacy‑safe aggregate data.
The first phase should be a minimum viable pilot in 2026–2027. This pilot does not need to replicate the Olympic environment. It should validate the core behaviour: will fans scan, play, finish and redeem? A suitable testbed is a domestic sporting event in Brisbane such as AFL, NRL, cricket or basketball, where natural waiting periods, visible screens and a strong local sponsor already exist. The minimum viable product should include QR‑code entry, guest mode, one branded mini‑game or quiz, a section‑based leaderboard, one redeemable concession or merchandise reward and a simple sponsor dashboard. Onboarding should be extremely light, with no app download, no mandatory account creation and a short path to the first interaction.
This phase should be evaluated through operational and commercial KPIs. Fan KPIs include scan rate, game start rate, completion rate, average time spent, repeat play, satisfaction score and opt‑in account creation. Commercial KPIs include sponsor impressions, interaction rate, cost per engaged fan, voucher redemption rate, incremental concession or merchandise sales and post‑game brand recall where feasible. Operational KPIs include page‑load time, Wi‑Fi reliability, scoreboard latency, helpdesk incidents and complaints. A pilot succeeds when Play the Games generates measurable engagement without slowing down venue operations or distracting from the sport.
The second phase, from 2028 to 2030, should scale the concept from a single pilot into a repeatable venue activation kit. The focus shifts from experimentation to standardisation. The platform should develop reusable game templates, sponsor modules, prize mechanics, data dashboards, privacy templates, legal terms and operational runbooks. Comparable innovations offer guidance. REDyPLAY shows that audiences can be activated through short interactive games before the main entertainment event. The LEGO campaign across 196 cinema screens demonstrates that such mechanics can scale and link directly to physical prize redemption (SAWA, 2022; Weischer, n.d.). Play the Games should translate this logic into sport by creating repeatable formats for queues, pre‑game windows, half‑time, weather delays and breaks between heats.
The commercial model in this phase should rely on sponsored activations rather than venue subscriptions. Brands can buy packages such as “Presented by” games, branded quizzes, reward sponsorships, leaderboard sponsorships or sport‑education moments. Venues provide access to screens, QR placement, Wi‑Fi support, concession integration and operational permissions. Revenue is shared between Play the Games and the venue or event owner. This model is easier to sell because it does not introduce a new cost line for venues. It unlocks a new revenue stream from attention that currently drifts to external platforms.
The third phase, from 2030 to 2031, prepares for Olympic integration. This requires governance, rights alignment and technical hardening. Brisbane 2032 will involve official partners, protected Olympic IP, category exclusivity and strict brand rights. Play the Games must operate inside the official sponsorship ecosystem. Only authorised partners should activate Olympic‑branded games. Category exclusivity must be respected. Beverage sponsors should own beverage‑related reward moments, while financial services, telecommunications or transport sponsors can activate different challenge types. This protects the Olympic commercial model and avoids sponsor conflict.
Technical readiness should mature in parallel. The platform can evolve from simple QR access to optional integration with ticketing systems, venue maps, digital wallets and account‑based points. Guest mode should remain because mandatory account creation increases friction and reduces inclusivity. The future‑state product can include cross‑venue leaderboards, multilingual rule explainers, accessibility settings, personalised reward paths and richer sponsor dashboards. The core experience stays simple: scan, play, learn, win, redeem.
Legal and ethical governance must be embedded from the start. Prize mechanics should favour skill‑based competitions such as quizzes, prediction accuracy, reaction games or judged creative answers. These formats are generally less complex than pure chance. Even so, they require transparent terms and conditions, accurate prize descriptions and compliance with Australian Consumer Law. Data governance should follow privacy‑by‑design principles. The platform should collect only what is needed, explain the value exchange clearly, allow guest participation, avoid selling personal data and use aggregated analytics for venue and sponsor reporting. Participation should remain free, opt‑in and inclusive.
The fourth phase is Games‑time deployment in 2032. The Games become the flagship proof of concept at scale. Deployment should begin with selected high‑volume venues and session types, then expand based on operational performance. Early use cases include pre‑session waiting time, breaks between heats, half‑time intervals, transport waiting zones and lower‑familiarity sports where rule explainers can improve understanding. During Games‑time, reliability and crowd‑safe experience matter more than feature complexity. Real‑time dashboards should monitor uptime, scan rates, server load, reward redemption, content performance and sponsor reporting.
The fifth phase is post‑2032 commercialisation. This is where the platform becomes a legacy product rather than a one‑off Olympic activation. The most realistic expansion path is through sports codes, venue groups and event owners. Early customers could include AFL clubs and stadium operators, NRL venues, Cricket Australia, A‑League clubs, Tennis Australia or multi‑event precincts. The pitch shifts from “help us test this” to “Brisbane 2032 proved this works; now license it for your season.” Commercialisation can follow a software‑as‑a‑service plus sponsorship marketplace model. Rights‑holders pay for the platform or share activation revenue, while brands pay for sponsored game packages.
This expansion requires a standard product architecture. Play the Games should offer a core platform, a content library, sport‑specific templates, sponsor modules, a redemption engine and analytics dashboards. Each new venue needs configuration rather than reinvention. Local content can adapt to the sport, sponsor, venue layout and fan base. The same mechanic works for cricket between overs, AFL quarter breaks, NRL half‑time, tennis changeovers, basketball timeouts and festival queues. The transferable asset is a repeatable engagement system for live‑event dead time.
A credible implementation plan also needs a learning loop. Each event should generate data that improves the next. The platform should analyse which game formats drive completion, which rewards drive redemption, which sports need more education, which time windows generate the highest participation and which sponsor categories produce the strongest response. This makes the product more valuable over time and creates a defensible advantage. The more venues and events use Play the Games, the stronger the benchmark data becomes.
In summary, Play the Games should be implemented as a staged, sponsor‑funded fan engagement platform. The first stage proves fan behaviour. The second standardises the product. The third aligns with Olympic governance. The fourth deploys at Brisbane 2032. The fifth turns the platform into a commercial product for Australian sport. This approach keeps risk manageable, gives venues a reason to participate without upfront cost, gives brands measurable engagement and gives fans a more rewarding and educational live‑sport experience. The long‑term ambition is that Brisbane 2032 becomes the proof point for a new category of sports sponsorship: playable attention.
